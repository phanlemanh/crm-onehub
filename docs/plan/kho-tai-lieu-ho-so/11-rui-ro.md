RISKS, FAILURE MODES AND CRITICISM of the LLM Wiki pattern and agent-maintained knowledge bases (research date 2026-09-26)
--- SUMMARY
Evidence is thin where it matters most. I found no controlled study of an agent-maintained wiki holding regulations or policies with a human approving each diff, and no first-person postmortem from a company team that abandoned one. Instead there are four kinds of evidence. (1) Measured results from adjacent settings: ACE shows a monolithic rewrite collapsing an 18,282-token context to 122 tokens; LLM summaries overgeneralize 26-73% of the time and drop scope qualifiers; RAG-based legal tools hallucinate 17-33% of the time; agent-memory error propagation is documented; LLM-written AGENTS.md files slightly hurt success and add over 20% cost. (2) One small preregistered wiki-vs-RAG study (Cochran, 24 papers), which found the wiki cost about 21x more tokens per query but gave better claim-level citation support, and had only one contradicted wiki claim (a one-word paraphrase artefact). (3) Hard first-hand bug reports from the largest open-source implementations, the most concrete evidence found: at 2,300-3,700 pages, whole-file rewrites corrupted the `sources` field of about 436 pages, a merge prompt leaked scaffolding into 92 files, fabricated example.com references appeared, and locking, auto-commit and truncation guards failed silently. (4) Opinion and vendor material that mostly restates the same handful of mechanisms: errors persist and become internally consistent ("false coherence"), lint checks wiki-to-wiki rather than wiki-to-source consistency, flat index.md navigation breaks at roughly 100-500 pages, and review becomes the new maintenance burden. The recurring pattern is that the failure is quiet. Pages stay fluent and well-linked while qualifiers, exact numbers and provenance erode, and the safety nets (locks, commits, lint, dry-runs, human approval) fail open or degrade under fatigue. The mitigations with the most support are structural rather than prompt-based: delta or patch updates instead of rewrites, deterministic validation of frontmatter and provenance, source hashes for staleness, quote-verification against the cited source, routing exact-figure questions to raw sources, and small bounded corpora. Almost all of these are reported by their own builders, not independently evaluated. The pattern is not shown to fail everywhere; several positive reports exist (a 1,440-page journalism wiki, three 2026 academic systems), but the positive reports either verify against originals by hand or measure QA benchmarks rather than the correctness of individual policy statements.
--- TENSIONS
 * Maintenance cost: Karpathy says maintenance cost is near zero and the LLM does everything except sourcing and questions; measured and reported figures show 100k+ tokens for one small source, about 21x higher query tokens in the one preregistered study, and a review burden that shifts to humans (nashsu #426, Anthropic's 93% approval).
 * Does the wiki hallucinate more than RAG? Critics argue yes because errors persist and reinforce; Cochran's small study had one contradicted wiki claim vs three for RAG and better claim-level citation support, while the Stanford study shows RAG tools hallucinate 17-33%. The defensible reading is a different risk profile (persistent, correlated, invisible to internal lint), not a proven higher rate.
 * Contradictions: Jim Liu says 'contradictions are assets' (keep both with contradicts: tags); Ghelbur says resolve rather than hoard; crajah and madgodinc say resolve at write time with declared exclusivity; Karpathy's gist says find them at lint time. No experiment compares these.
 * Compression vs bloat: ACE shows rewrites can collapse content to a fraction (18,282 to 122 tokens), while nashsu and blurman show accumulation growing unbounded or pages exceeding their sources. The two mitigations (delta updates vs deterministic bounded pages) pull in different directions.
 * Verify or trust: blurman finds a wiki that must be re-verified against source on every read saves nothing, and Newton verifies against originals by hand; critics such as Kumar and frosk1 say the wiki must never replace raw sources. Trusting fully at query time requires the freshness and quote-verification machinery.
 * Is model collapse relevant? devnullbrain cites it against wikis; ChrisGreenHeur and gojomo say it concerns training, and self-generated data is now productive. ACE and error-propagation studies are the better-grounded in-context analogues.
 * Where does it break? Claims range from about 100-200 pages (Kumar, dhruvil-1990, skynet) to about 1,000 files (anonymous gist commenters, secondhand summaries) to working at 1,440 pages (Newton) and about 7,500 typed notes (distorx). The variable appears to be architecture (whole-file rewrites, flat index, structure enforcement), not page count.
 * Is it a wiki at all? Anonymous gist commenters call an LLM-written folder a category error because no humans collaborate; OneHub's design restores human authorship and approval, which moves it away from Karpathy's LLM-owned pages and out of the evidence base for the pattern.
 * Human-in-the-loop as fix vs as failure point: nearly every source recommends human approval, while Anthropic's 93% approval rate, Lee et al. and the Ar9av dry-run example show approval degrades with volume and confidence.
 * Source independence: many of the most detailed failure descriptions and fixes come from vendors (Penfield, Neo4j, agentmemory, AMS, Link, Cortex) selling a remedy, so their numbers and framing are self-interested.
--- IMPLICATIONS
 > Inference: the planned design (page owner approves every diff; the company agent answers only from approved pages and cites them) is the same mitigation the critics converge on (review-gated, citation-first, per laphilosophia, Kumar and innobu). It is necessary but not sufficient, because the main risks are omissions and drift that a diff of the wiki page alone does not reveal.
 > Inference: make the review screen show each changed claim beside the exact source passage (document, version, page, quote) and require an explicit per-claim tick for numbers, dates, thresholds, conditions and exceptions. This targets the qualifier-loss and 'in 95%' to 'over 95%' failures.
 > Inference: make provenance a machine-checked field, not prose: every claim carries source document ID, a content hash of the source version, and a quote; refuse to save a page whose quotes do not appear in the cited source (Cortex-style check); keep raw sources immutable. nashsu #536 and #783 show prose or LLM-written provenance gets corrupted or fabricated.
 > Inference: split content by risk. Exact figures, fees, legal clauses, effective dates and contract terms should be verbatim quotes with anchors or answered from raw sources at query time (Kumar's routing rule and nashsu's 'Raw sources only' mode). Use agent synthesis for glossaries, cross-document summaries and decisions, where a derived page adds something not readable in one source (AGENTS.md study).
 > Inference: build staleness into the data model. Store source hash, effective_from, superseded_by and a review-by date per page type, mark a page stale automatically when its source changes, and have the Q&A agent state that a page is stale. Amended regulations produce the 'faithful to a stale version' failure that no fluency check catches.
 > Inference: forbid whole-page rewrites by the agent. Accept only section-level patch proposals against a base version, reject proposals whose base changed, cap how many unrelated sections a proposal may touch, lock human-authored sections against agent edits, and keep append-only history. ACE, nashsu #536 and WikiLoom all point the same way; because OneHub pages are human-owned, the 'regenerate from scratch' fallback recommended by Proudfrog is not available.
 > Inference: fail loudly. Treat 'the agent says it saved' as no evidence; verify the row or commit exists; reject and flag truncated model output; show per run how many pages were touched, tokens used and any truncation; alert if a proposal was silently dropped. The tools reviewed all had silent failures in their locks, commits, truncation guards or queues.
 > Inference: review load is the real running cost. Give every page a named owner and a backup, tier pages by risk (policy, legal, finance require owner approval; low-risk glossary can be lighter), dedupe near-identical proposals, cap open proposals per owner, and track approval rate and time-to-approve as an early warning (93% approval in Anthropic's data; nashsu's review pile-up). Add periodic audits of approved pages against sources (Kumar: 10 pages weekly; Proudfrog: 5 claims weekly).
 > Inference: constrain the company agent. It answers only from approved pages, cites page and claim, and says 'not in the wiki' instead of falling back to model memory; keep retrieval packs small (fabrication rises with context length); do not let an index-only shortcut permit answering without reading a page; test Vietnamese queries with and without diacritics on the retriever and lint; check whether agent-drafted pages outrank human-written ones (source bias).
 > Inference: treat the ingest agent as untrusted with respect to source text. Give it read-only access to sources, no network or tools beyond emitting a proposal, delimit source text as data, log every proposal, and begin with trusted internal sources; treat external PDFs, emails and meeting transcripts as higher risk. The human diff review is the main control, and OWASP says prevention is not guaranteed.
 > Inference: set cost guardrails before launch: per-run and daily token caps, tokens per approved page as a tracked metric, deterministic generation of index and link pages, and delta-only updates. Do not assume the 'near zero' maintenance cost claim; measured examples show 100k+ tokens for one small source and about 21x query cost in the one preregistered comparison.
 > Inference: build an evaluation set early: 50-100 real questions with gold answers and source citations, pinned to a corpus version, rerun on every model, prompt or schema change, and compared with a raw-source retrieval baseline. Cochran's decomposition-RAG variant removed most of the wiki's synthesis advantage, so measure uplift rather than assume it.
 > Inference: start with a bounded corpus, plan hybrid or full-text search from day one (flat index navigation is reported to break at roughly 100-500 pages), and validate frontmatter and entity aliases with deterministic checks in CI or on write. Structure at the write path is the most consistently reported working mitigation, though only self-reported.
 > Inference: decide the human-plus-agent authorship protocol explicitly. Karpathy's pattern is LLM-owned pages ('You never (or rarely) write the wiki yourself'); OneHub lets staff write and edit. That is outside the evidence found, so the protocol for concurrent human and agent edits (proposal against a base version, section ownership, conflict reject) needs to be designed and tested rather than borrowed.
--- OPEN
 ? No study was found that measures whether human reviewers, shown an LLM-drafted wiki diff, catch dropped qualifiers, wrong numbers or fabricated citations. This is the central assumption of the OneHub design and remains untested here.
 ? No verified in-the-wild incident of an LLM wiki poisoned through an ingested source was found; injection evidence is lab attacks on RAG and memory plus small threat-model projects.
 ? No first-person postmortem from a company team that adopted and then abandoned an agent-maintained wiki was found; abandonment evidence is partial retreats.
 ? The WebSearch budget (200 calls) was exhausted mid-session, so follow-ups were limited to direct fetches: Reddit threads, Vietnamese-language sources, and possible papers on document corruption under delegated LLM editing were not searched.
 ? Several critiques could not be opened (connection resets): Anand Lahoti's 'The Hidden Flaw in Karpathy's LLM Wiki', Mehul Gupta's 'is a Bad Idea', Tom Nguyen's work-use account, and R&D World's one-month review (403). I saw them only as search snippets, so I did not rely on them.
 ? The Nature model-collapse page redirected to an auth flow; I used the arXiv version. The Effortless Academic's 'users follow wrong AI about 80% of the time' figure was not traced to a primary source.
 ? Claims read via a summarising fetch tool rather than full text (Cochran's 40.2% vs 18.9% and the 'over 95%' example, the AGENTS.md cost figures, Wikipedia policy dating, the Grokipedia timeline) may contain small errors; the Cochran abstract and the GitHub issue and comment bodies were read verbatim.
 ? Eyaldavid7's claim in the gist that a 'mostly finished' wiki performed 17% worse than a fully compiled one (130 questions, code repo) links to a Substack I did not read, so it is unverified.
 ? Issue-tracker figures (762 occurrences, 436 corrupted pages, 9,090 false findings) are single-reporter audits of specific tools and versions; whether a custom OneHub implementation would reproduce them is unknown.
 ? Vietnamese-specific behaviour of tokenization, diacritic folding, retrieval and lint is unverified; only CJK and German analogues were found.
 ? The Cochran wiki build's human review, if any, is not disclosed, and all scoring was by LLM judges; effects of a human-approved wiki on its error rate are unmeasured.
 ? Jim Liu's article has an internal date inconsistency (title 'Six Months In', dated 2026-05-06, a month after the gist), so its timeline claims are low confidence.
