====================================================================================================
Human-in-the-loop governance and review UX for an AI-authored, owner-approved knowledge wiki
--- SUMMARY
The evidence says a human approval gate does not, by itself, produce review. Approval rates run near 93-97% for agent permission prompts, and in Anthropic's own injected-danger test humans caught the dangerous command 13.6% of the time, falling to about 5% after 50+ prior prompts. Automation-bias research (clinical, radiology, pathology, code review) shows the same pattern across experts and novices. Explanations, confidence scores and citations mostly raise trust rather than accuracy. The interventions with the best evidence are structural: making the reviewer commit an independent judgment before seeing the AI verdict (Fogliato 2022; Buçinca 2021, though forcing is disliked and can overload), aggregating independent human and AI judgments, accountability for accuracy (Skitka; Goddard review), small time-boxed review units (Cisco/Google), and instrumenting the queue with seeded errors and catch-rate metrics. The knowledge-base analogs are instructive: Wikipedia's pending-changes reviewers are explicitly scoped to not vouch for correctness, German Wikipedia's review-everything flagged revisions averaged a 15-day delay, and English Wikipedia banned LLM-authored article content in 2026 because it arrives at a rate that makes review infeasible. Commercial KB verification (Guru, Notion) gives a workable pattern of named owner, expiry interval, reminder, and unverified state, but its escalation and absent-owner handling is thin or undocumented. LLM-wiki implementations diverge sharply: Karpathy's own pattern and the Astro-Han implementation have no review gate, Vouch and Falconer add owner-review and mechanical citation checks, and the critique literature (Joi Ito, LLM Wiki v2 comments) flags silent overwrite of contradictions and false-precision confidence numbers as the main gaps. Most of the transferable evidence is by analogy; I found no controlled study of reviewers approving LLM-drafted wiki diffs, so design recommendations for OneHub are labelled inference and should be validated with seeded-error measurement in its own queue. The session web-search budget ran out before some planned angles (OpenAI/Microsoft agent-approval products, DeepMind delegation paper, Reddit/Meta moderation queues) were covered.
--- TENSIONS
 * Approval gates versus classifiers: Anthropic argues per-action human approval has decayed into rubber-stamping (93-97% approval; 13.6% catch of an injected danger) and replaces most prompts with an LLM classifier, but its own full-pipeline false-negative rate on real overeager actions was 17%, a third-party reviewer says auto mode is not suitable for high-stakes tasks, and an analyst frames the 13.6% as proof the metric was flawed from the start. The test was vendor-run and framed around a disguised command, which may favour the classifier (my inference).
 * Do explanations, confidence and citations help? Microsoft-era overreliance reviews and Zhang et al. report confidence and explanations can help calibrate trust, while Bansal (explanations raise acceptance regardless of correctness), Romeo and Conti (35 studies: explanations often insufficient), Ding (citations raise trust even when random) and Gaube (limited effectiveness) say they mostly raise trust, not accuracy. Chen et al. found confidence and simple visuals helped in one high-stakes task. The disagreement is partly about task and expertise, and I found no wiki-review study.
 * Forcing functions: Buçinca shows cognitive forcing reduces overreliance most but users rate it worst and only high-need-for-cognition users benefit strongly; Chen et al. found engagement-heavy prompts sometimes reduced performance through overload; Fogliato found asking for a provisional answer first lowered agreement with the AI without adding time. Friction design is task-sensitive.
 * Human plus AI is not reliably better: Vaccaro's meta-analysis finds decision tasks lose against the best of human or AI alone, while regulation (EU AI Act Art. 14) and accountability logic insist on human override, and Elish's moral crumple zone shows the human bears blame regardless. The wiki gate's justification is accountability and local knowledge, not raw accuracy (my inference).
 * Numeric confidence scores: LLM Wiki v2 and some implementers want per-claim scores with decay; a v2 commenter and the Astro-Han project call them false precision and prefer evidence chains; one operator keeps only three tags (stated, high, low). The evidence on calibrated confidence (Zhang) is conditional on domain expertise.
 * Whether to gate at all: Karpathy's pattern and Astro-Han ship without review machinery because one person is curator and reader; Vouch, Falconer, MindBase and Cortex treat the gate as the point; a v2 commenter reports silent corruption when models write without a human handoff.
 * Review everything versus tier: German Wikipedia's default flagging averaged a 15-day delay with disputed benefit, and English Wikipedia dropped its review-all level, while DORA finds heavyweight external approval slows delivery without lowering failure rates. Countervailing: Article 14 and the AI-hallucination court record push toward more human verification in legal and regulatory content.
 * One reviewer or two: Rigby and Bird's convergent practices (cited by Sadowski) put two reviewers as optimal for defects, Google runs mostly one for speed, Hybrid Confirmation Tree uses a second human only on human-AI disagreement, and the two-person rule literature warns of mutual complacency.
 * Verification stamps and edits: Guru treats verifier edits as implicit verification, GitHub invalidates approvals when the diff changes, and Notion's docs are silent. These encode opposite trade-offs between low friction and stamp integrity.
 * Does checking sources help? Users who verified citations trusted LLM answers less (Ding), but participants who did online research became more confident in wrong answers (confirmation-bias oversight study). Verification helps only when it is genuinely evidence-seeking rather than confirmatory.
 * Approval-rate statistic disagreement: Anthropic's March engineering post says 93% of permission prompts are approved, its August blog says 97%. Both are cited as evidence of habitual clicking; the underlying change or measurement date was not established.
--- IMPLICATIONS
 > Inference: define exactly what each stamp attests, because the strongest KB precedent (Wikipedia) narrows reviewer scope so a stamp never overclaims. Use at least two distinct meanings: 'source-checked' (each claim matches its cited passage) and 'owner-confirmed current' (still matches how OneHub actually operates), with the owner's name, date and scope visible on the page and in every agent citation.
 > Inference: tier by downstream consequence of the page content, not by size of the edit. Suggested tiers: (0) auto-apply with audit log and one-click revert for typos, formatting, link and backlink repair; (1) single named owner review for normal content updates from new sources; (2) maker-checker (two named people) for policy, regulation, commission, pricing and compliance pages. Any edit that resolves a contradiction or removes a claim is at least tier 1. Cheap reversibility of wiki edits supports auto-apply at tier 0, but the risk sits in what the answering agent reads, so it must read only the last approved revision (Wikipedia stable-revision pattern; Vouch retrieval rule).
 > Inference: do not ask the owner to judge the model's verdict. Show information, not a recommendation (Goddard review): the reviewer sees the source passage first, the claim, and a rendered diff; no pre-selected Approve, no agent confidence or 'looks good' before the reviewer has recorded their own read. For tier 1-2, require a one-line per-claim answer ('what does the source say about X?') or a provisional accept/reject before the agent's wording is revealed (Fogliato 2022; Buçinca 2021), and keep this friction proportional because forcing functions are disliked and can overload (Buçinca; Chen et al.).
 > Inference: package review as a change set per ingest, with a hard cap tied to reviewer capacity. Karpathy notes one source can touch 10-15 pages; per-page approvals would multiply fatigue. Show one card per claim change ordered by risk, target roughly 200-400 lines-equivalent or under an hour per sitting (Cisco), have the agent write a short 'why I changed this' note per claim (author annotation helps in code review), and throttle ingestion when the queue is above a threshold. Wikipedia banned LLM article authoring because generation outpaced review; rate control is a first-class control.
 > Inference: run mechanical checks before any human sees the proposal and keep them out of the reviewer's judgment load. Quote-exists-in-source (Claude API Citations char offsets; Vouch content-hash check), link/backlink validity, duplicate detection and conflict detection against existing claims should gate entry to the queue. Do not treat a valid citation as a support check, since citations raise trust without proving support (Ding 2025; Liu 2023); the human check should be 'does the quoted passage actually say this'.
 > Inference: show conflicts as a decision card, never as a silent overwrite. Present the existing claim and the new claim side by side, each with source quote, document date, effective date and authority level; the owner picks one of: supersede (old kept as historical with an end date, Wikidata-style qualifier/rank), keep both with a scope qualifier (product, date range, audience), reject the new source, or escalate. Show the two sides in a neutral order and require the reviewer to choose, since two-sided presentation helped only when the model was wrong (2507.19486).
 > Inference: assign exactly one accountable owner per page plus one named deputy, and reject ownerless pages (Notion requires an owner; Falconer flags the missing-curator failure; Gaube's hiring case shows diffusion of responsibility with two overseers). Group verifiers in Guru's model put the task in every member's queue, which invites bystander effects; use a rotating single assignee instead.
 > Inference: build absent-owner escalation as a timeout ladder with visible clocks. Owner, then deputy (auto-skipping people marked out of office, as GitHub's team assignment does), then area lead, then admin, with a repeat limit and a manual fallback (PagerDuty model). Add Nudge-style reminders, which cut stalled PR resolution time by 60% at Microsoft. Suggested first-response SLA of one business day for tier 1 and two for tier 2, from Google's one-business-day rule; these numbers are starting points to tune.
 > Inference: make stamps expire and invalidate on change. Set expiry by risk (for example 30/90/180 days), notify the owner, flag overdue pages in the UI and in agent answers, and demote overdue pages (Guru and Notion both boost or flag by verification status). Unlike Guru's implicit verify-on-edit, invalidate the stamp on any substantive change, following GitHub's dismiss-stale-approvals design, so a small edit cannot inherit a full-page stamp.
 > Inference: measure review quality directly, not by approval counts. Inject a small fraction of seeded flawed proposals (wrong figure, quote not supporting claim, superseded regulation) and track catch rate over time and by queue position, as Anthropic did for command approvals (13.6% catch, ~5% after 50+ prompts). Also track time-per-item and dwell on the source panel, edit-before-approve rate, rejection and override reasons, post-approval revert or correction rate, queue age and SLA breach rate, share of pages with a valid stamp, and agent-answer complaints traced back to specific pages. Report these to the owners as quality feedback, not as productivity targets (Gaube: speed targets undermine oversight).
 > Inference: use accountability and role clarity as the cheapest debiasing lever. Skitka and the Goddard review link accountability for accuracy to lower automation bias. Store named approver, tier and stamp on the page, and give reviewers an explicit right and easy path to reject, edit or ask the agent to redo (LangChain-style approve/edit/reject/respond); rejection must be as cheap as approval or approving becomes the path of least resistance.
 > Inference: for high-risk pages, consider independent-first double review: the owner records their own reading, then sees the agent's diff, and only disagreements go to a second person (Hybrid Confirmation Tree; Fogliato). Avoid naive two-person approval where each assumes the other checked (two-person rule complacency), and use an approver different from whoever triggered the ingest, as GitHub does for agent-authored PRs.
 > Inference: label AI involvement precisely on pages. Say 'drafted by the agent from source X, reviewed by <owner> on <date>' rather than a bare 'AI-generated' badge, which lowers belief mainly when people assume full automation (Altay & Gilardi). Use plain-language uncertainty for flagged claims ('I'm not sure the source says this...') rather than numeric confidence, which practitioners and the evidence treat as false precision unless calibrated.
 > Inference: keep the Vietnamese regulatory context first-class. Regulation-derived pages should carry issuing body, document number, effective date and validity status, and contradictions between an old and new document (amendment, replacement, expiry) should be the default conflict case in the review UI. I did not verify Vietnamese legal-database status conventions, so this needs a local check.
--- OPEN
 ? No controlled study was found on reviewers approving LLM-drafted wiki or policy diffs; every design recommendation transfers by analogy from code review, clinical decision support, content moderation and agent approvals.
 ? Optimal seeded-error rate, its effect on reviewer morale, and whether seeding should be disclosed in advance were not established; the only large seeded-error measurement found is Anthropic's vendor study on command approval.
 ? Whether a rendered prose diff, a claim-level card view, or a source-anchored side-by-side is best for reviewers of regulatory text is unresolved; I found only GitHub's rich diff (comments only in source view) and no user study. File-position effects in code review (Fregnan et al.) were not verified.
 ? The 97% vs 93% permission-prompt approval figures from two Anthropic posts were not reconciled.
 ? Romeo and Conti's 35-study review, Bansal et al., Skitka et al. and the Copilot 'requester approval does not count' rule were confirmed only through abstracts, search snippets or tool summaries; full texts were paywalled or blocked (403/redirects).
 ? Secondary claims traced only to blogs (HBS 2024 study with 228 evaluators, 'AI agent traps' DeepMind 2025, TianPan case studies on insurance denials and Durham policing) were not verified and are excluded from the findings as facts.
 ? Guru's behaviour when a verifier leaves, and Notion's behaviour when a verified page is edited, are not documented in the pages I fetched; competitor escalation mechanics for absent knowledge owners remain unknown beyond generic on-call and reviewer-assignment patterns.
 ? Vietnamese legal-document status conventions (in force, partly expired, not yet in force) and how they map to a page status model were not researched.
 ? The session's web-search budget (200 searches) ran out mid-research, so OpenAI Agents SDK and Microsoft agent-approval UX, Google DeepMind's delegation work, Reddit/Meta moderation queues, Confluence page approvals, and reviewer-side studies of diff ordering were not covered.
